                                               Abstract
            The growing popularity of generative AI tools presents new challenges for data
        quality in online surveys and experiments. This study examines research participants’
        use of large language models to answer open-ended survey questions and describes
        empirical tendencies in human vs LLM-generated text responses. In an original survey
        of participants recruited from a popular online platform for sourcing social science
        research subjects, 34% reported using LLMs to help them answer open-ended survey
        questions. Simulations comparing human-written responses from three pre-ChatGPT
        studies with LLM-generated text reveal that LLM responses are more homogeneous
        and positive, particularly when they describe social groups in sensitive questions. These
        homogenization patterns may mask important underlying social variation in attitudes
        and beliefs among human subjects, raising concerns about data validity. Our findings
        shed light on the scope and potential consequences of participants’ LLM use in online
        research.

1    Introduction

The rise of web-based, self-administered surveys and online experiments in social science research
has created valuable new opportunities for researchers but raises concerns about data quality. Issues
such as participant inattention, disengagement, and dishonesty are often amplified in these formats
(Alvarez et al., 2019; Ternovski and Orr, 2022; Bell and Gift, 2023). Popular online platforms like
Amazon Mechanical Turk and Prolific present additional challenges, including automated bots,
multiple submissions, and cross-contamination between studies (Chmielewski and Kucker, 2020;
Douglas et al., 2023). Researchers have worked to measure these issues and develop mitigation
strategies to improve data quality (Berinsky et al., 2014; Cuskley and Sulik, 2024).
    In this article, we add to this work by examining an emerging challenge in online data collec-
tion: research participants’ use of generative AI. The release of low-cost, public-facing AI tools
like ChatGPT has spurred their rapid adoption in professional settings and among the general pub-
lic. Powered by large language models (LLMs), these commercial AI tools have exhibited strong
performance on tasks such as producing natural-sounding original text, offering writing assistance,
and generating ideas. Scholars have already started examining the implications of generative AI
tools in arenas like education and the workplace (e.g. Smolansky et al., 2023; Dell’Acqua et al.,
2023; Brynjolfsson et al., 2023).
    This article extends these lines of inquiry to generative AI’s implications for web-based re-
search. We examine participants’ use of AI when answering open-ended text questions, a place
where text-generation tools may prove especially disruptive. Open-ended questions appear widely
across social science study designs. In surveys and experiments – the focus of the present paper –
they are used to deliver interventions or experimental treatments, measure outcomes, probe the rea-
sons or mechanisms behind close-ended choices, and assess the validity of other measures (Ziegler,
2022; Ferrario and Stantcheva, 2022; Hadler, 2023; Landesvatter and Bauer, 2024). Open-ended
responses are also a popular object of analysis in the literature on text as data (Roberts et al., 2014;
Egami et al., 2022) and used in computational social science for training and evaluating machine


                                                   1
learning models. While valuable for researchers, study participants report finding open-ended
questions burdensome and time-consuming (Fowler et al., 2023). Online study participants, who
usually receive flat payments as compensation, are incentivized to finish studies quickly to max-
imize their earnings. Against this backdrop, emerging evidence from crowdsourcing platforms
suggests that some online research participants are turning to generative AI to answer writing-
intensive questions (Veselovsky et al., 2023a,b).
   This article offers a first descriptive portrait of participants’ use of LLMs to answer open-
ended questions. We fielded a survey on Prolific, a popular online survey platform, to measure
the self-reported prevalence of LLM use, understand participants’ reasons for using or not using
LLMs, characterize the prompting strategies participants employ, and describe variations in LLM
use among subgroups.
   We then investigate the potential consequences of this emerging practice for the quality, con-
tent, and variance of survey responses, with the aim of informing researchers’ expectations about
how participants’ LLM use may shape study results. Extant research suggests participants’ use of
LLMs may raise several potential issues. Most immediately, when participants generate responses
to open-text questions, those answers can diverge from what respondents would have written ab-
sent AI tools. While some scholarship on how well carefully fine-tuned LLMs can predict survey
answers has suggested LLM-generated responses are sometimes consistent with average human
responses (Kim and Lee, 2023; Bisbee et al., 2024; Kozlowski et al., 2024; Ashokkumar et al.,
2024), it is far less clear whether the ways in which research participants use off-the-shelf genera-
tive AI tools yield responses that align with their actual perspectives, rather than those encoded as
generic positions in LLMs. Other research suggests that LLMs – whether as text generators or as
writing assistants – tend to produce or steer people towards answers that are more homogeneous in
style and content than natural human writing (Padmakumar and He, 2023; Anderson et al., 2024).
Consequently, LLM-influenced study responses may mask important forms of social variation.
   We evaluate these possibilities by assessing the extent to which LLM-produced responses align
with and diverge from human-generated responses. To do so, we draw on open-ended questions


                                                    2
posed in three studies fielded as part of the Time-Sharing Experiments of the Social Sciences
(TESS) program prior to the public release of ChatGPT. We compare those responses to LLM-
simulated responses to the same questions. Our simulations are informed by our survey, in which
we asked online survey takers how they would prompt an AI chatbot to answer each of the three
questions.
   We find that 34% of Prolific participants report using LLMs at least some of the time when
they answer open-ended questions, with 9% reporting using LLMs “most of the time” or “always.”
We also find that LLM usage varies along demographic dimensions that correlate with cleavages
in public opinion, which could bias estimates of between-group differences. Specifically, Black,
male, college-educated, Republican, and participants newer to the Prolific platform are more likely
to report using LLMs in our survey, though these patterns may change as AI technology diffuses
more widely. Those who use LLMs most commonly cite needing help expressing their thoughts,
AI’s speed and writing quality, and researchers providing unclear instructions as reasons for turn-
ing to LLMs. Their most common prompting strategies include asking AI to generate ideas or
provide additional information on the topic. Notably, perspective-taking prompts that incorporate
participants’ pre-existing views or social positions are rare. Meanwhile, those who refrain from
using LLMs often cite moral reasons related to authenticity, ethical concerns about “cheating,” or
compliance with rules.
   Comparing LLM-generated responses with human responses recorded in TESS data, we find
that LLM-written text has a larger vocabulary but exhibits greater homogeneity across responses.
The substance and tone of LLM-generated text also depart sharply from human-written answers,
with LLM-generated answers using more positive language. In sensitive questions about group
perceptions and stereotypes, human-written responses are more likely to feature dehumanizing
language than LLM-simulated responses. Overall, our results show that free-text survey responses
written by humans convey a broader range of views than answers generated by LLMs.
   Together, our results provide an initial view of the emerging landscape and the consequences
of study participants’ use of LLMs. We present data on the scope and distribution of this practice


                                                3
among study participants. Drawing on simulated LLM answers, we outline ways AI use could
affect conclusions drawn from online surveys and experiments, while identifying directions for
future research to further investigate the consequences of this evolving development. We con-
clude by identifying implications for research practice and connecting the study’s results to related
literatures on silicon sampling, AI evaluation, and the societal impacts of AI.



2    Generative AI and Human Subjects Surveys and Experiments

As generative AI use spreads among the general public, scholars and observers have raised ques-
tions about the implications of these tools for how people write and express themselves across
many domains of social life, from peer review to medicine to the arts (Epstein et al., 2023; Liang
et al., 2024; Tai-Seale et al., 2024). Recent studies of crowd-working platforms that social scientists
often rely on to recruit research participants suggest that the consequences of LLMs extend to how
participants engage with online studies. For instance, two studies of participants on Amazon Me-
chanical Turk (MTurk) and Prolific found that substantial shares – between 33-46% depending on
how one infers LLM usage – relied on an LLM to complete a text summarization task (Veselovsky
et al., 2023a,b). In a survey of crowdworkers fielded in December 2023, 13% of Prolific, 15% of
Clickworker, 73% of MTurk participants reported ever using an AI chatbot to complete a crowd-
sourcing task (Christoforou et al., 2024). This article builds on this prior work measuring AI use in
crowd-working tasks broadly, focusing specifically on the implications of AI use in social science
surveys and experiments where it can influence the measurement of human subjects’ subjective
beliefs, attitudes, experiences, and behaviors.
    Research participants’ use of AI shares similarities with past challenges researchers have faced
in online studies, but also presents unique considerations. LLM usage resembles previously ex-
amined issues such as bots in online surveys (Chmielewski and Kucker, 2020) and respondents’
information search behaviors during studies (Clifford and Jerit, 2016; Gummer and Kunz, 2022;
Graham, 2023). Like these earlier challenges, LLM use consists of an external influence on study



                                                  4
participation that can undermine the authenticity of participants’ responses and compromise the
validity of research findings. Researchers have developed various solutions for these past issues,
such as attention checks and response time analysis (Berinsky et al., 2014; Kennedy et al., 2020),
which may inform strategies for addressing LLM use. However, LLMs are also distinct in impor-
tant ways. Unlike bots, which are typically deployed to game survey systems, LLMs can be used
by real human participants as assistive tools and raise novel questions about the authenticity and
validity of responses. Further, while information search traditionally involves participants actively
looking up external content to inform their answers, LLMs can generate answers with less active
effort on the part of participants.
   In this article, we investigate the consequences of subjects’ LLM use by focusing on the case of
open-ended text questions, writing-intensive components of studies for which participants might
be most likely to turn to LLMs. When open-ended text responses are used for measurement –
whether to capture outcomes or mechanisms, or to probe other answers further – participants’
use of LLMs raises important questions about how they might shape the natural responses that
participants would give (Davidson, 2024).
   In the ideal, LLMs could offer benefits that are aligned with researchers’ objectives of obtaining
valid, unbiased results. AI writing assistance could make it easier and faster to write out fleshed-
out responses or help participants more precisely express their true underlying beliefs or attitudes.
Research on the implications of LLM use in other domains, for instance, suggests that LLMs may
be especially beneficial for study participants with weaker writing skills (Noy and Zhang, 2023;
Mollick, 2023; Brynjolfsson et al., 2023). To the extent that LLMs lower burdens on participant
time and effort without compromising measurement validity, LLM use among participants may
generate other ancillary benefits, like minimizing respondent fatigue, item non-response, and sur-
vey attrition (Peytchev, 2009; Zuell et al., 2015).
   But emerging evidence on the values and perspectives embedded in LLMs raises the possibility
that participants’ interactions with them could distort answers and compromise data quality. If par-
ticipants directly copy LLM-generated responses or craft answers informed by LLM suggestions


                                                  5
(Jakesch et al., 2023), how might that influence answers gathered in response to open-ended survey
questions? A rapidly growing body of research has aimed to characterize LLM tendencies when
prompted to respond to attitudinal and behavioral questions ordinarily posed to humans. While
much of this work was developed to address other concerns, such as probing the social biases or
point of view that LLMs encode (Santurkar et al., 2023; Motoki et al., 2024) or evaluating how
well LLMs can simulate human attitudes and actions in “silicon samples” (Boelaert et al., 2024;
Bail, 2024), it offers important clues as to how study participants’ exposure to LLM assistance
could impact their answers in surveys and experiments.
   A key lesson from this prior work is that LLM-provided answers to survey questions can be
highly sensitive to how LLMs are prompted (Wright et al., 2024). Users may prompt LLMs,
for instance, to offer a generic position on a question, such as “What is a reason to support or
oppose gun control?” Studies on the default positions that publicly available, commercial LLMs
tend to generate find that they most closely reflect the positions of particular subpopulations, such
as people in the US and some countries in Europe and South America (Durmus et al., 2023) and
more left-leaning individuals (Santurkar et al., 2023; Hartmann et al., 2023; Martin, 2023; Rozado,
2023; Motoki et al., 2024). This suggests that, when prompted in this manner, LLM-assisted
answers may steer some respondents away from their true positions and toward the generic views
of LLMs.
   Users may also prompt LLMs in more tailored ways, such as by signaling their own stances
on an issue or providing additional detail on their backgrounds, such as “I’m a progressive woman
from the Midwest. What is a reason to support gun control?” This latter approach approximates
a common strategy that researchers employ to study how accurately LLMs can predict human re-
sponses. In such studies, researchers typically supply LLMs with background information about
a hypothetical respondent to assemble a persona that is then used to simulate responses. Some
studies suggest that LLMs can reasonably match average human opinions and positions in specific
contexts (Argyle et al., 2023; Kozlowski et al., 2024; Kim and Lee, 2023; Bisbee et al., 2024;
Ashokkumar et al., 2024). Closest to our setting, Argyle et al. (2023) show that when prompted


                                                 6
with sociodemographic profiles of hypothetical respondents, LLMs produce similar words to de-
scribe Republicans and Democrats as do real respondents matching those demographics. Other
studies meanwhile suggest that LLMs can exhibit tendencies toward misportraying or caricaturing
the positions of members of distinct social groups (Wang et al., 2024; Cheng et al., 2023; Durmus
et al., 2023; Neumann et al., 2024) 1 Overall, however, this literature suggests that such persona-
based prompting may lead to AI-informed answers that are closer to participants’ true answers
than would more generic prompting strategies.
    Yet, despite the significance of prompting strategies, we know little about how study partici-
pants engage with AI when answering open-ended questions. In this study, we address this gap by
directly asking study participants who use AI to draft sample prompts they would use to help them
answer survey questions. While researchers use sophisticated methods to simulate answers, in-
cluding careful prompt engineering and fine-tuning to mimic human responses (Ollion et al., 2023;
Jensen et al., 2022), most participants are unlikely to engage with AI in such tailored or detailed
ways. For many, drafting such prompts may be more burdensome than simply answering the ques-
tion directly. By examining the types of responses that participant-written prompts produce, this
study reveals what participants are exposed to when relying on AI, shedding light on the direction
in which AI interactions may influence participant answers.
    Beyond congruence between the average or typical responses of LLMs and humans, additional
research highlights the possibility that LLMs may suppress natural human variation, homogenizing
or standardizing respondents’ answers in both content and style. LLMs embed the perspectives
present in their training data, as well as the commitments and concerns of their developers (Bender
et al., 2021; Alvero and Peña, 2023). Despite efforts by LLM developers to “democratize” design
decisions and incorporate diverse text in their training corpora, LLMs commonly learn and reflect
dominant cultural styles and perspectives, such as those held by demographic majorities or groups
over-represented in cultural production (Cao et al., 2023; Naous et al., 2024; Tao et al., 2024;
Gillespie, 2024). It is also the case that many LLMs, including the most popular platforms where

users can interface with them via chatbot, are not scientific but rather commercial products: having
a product that can give consistent, reliable results may be better for some business and industrial
scientific applications. However, it can present a hazard for surveys and experiments where LLM-
induced homogenization can lead to misleading or incorrect conclusions.
   A variety of studies have highlighted the potential of LLMs to homogenize people’s positions
and written self-expressions. LLMs tend to predict that opinions and attitudes within social groups
are more uniform than they are in reality (Santurkar et al., 2023; Park et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024;
Bisbee et al., 2024). They also tend to produce writing with particular styles and word choices.
Wright et al. (2024), for example, show that LLM open responses to political questions often
repeat similar linguistic tropes, even across prompting strategies, models, and political stances.
Another study comparing human-written and LLM-generated college admissions essays also finds
that the writing styles of LLMs exhibit much less variation than the writing styles of humans within
different social groups (Alvero et al., 2024). Liang et al. (2024) similarly show that academic peer
reviews with linguistic markers consistent with being AI-generated tend to be more homogeneous
and formulaic. Experimental research on the effects of LLM-based writing assistants further offers
evidence that LLMs steer human authors toward writing opinion essays and stories that are less
diverse (Padmakumar and He, 2023; Doshi and Hauser, 2024). Thus, we might expect that LLM
use among research participants – whether to generate answers directly or to inform human-written
answers – could lead to data that obscures social variation.
   In sum, while the growing body of research on LLMs provides important insights into the capa-
bilities and limitations of these tools, there remains a significant gap in understanding how their use
by research participants might influence the quality and validity of textual data collected in online
surveys and experiments. Our study addresses this gap by providing the first empirical portrait of
LLM use among online study participants, examining its prevalence, participants’ motivations for
using LLMs, and their prompting strategies when seeking AI assistance. Furthermore, we extend
prior research by simulating LLM-generated responses based on the prompting strategies reported
by actual online research participants, rather than using researcher-designed prompts. By com-


                                                   8
paring these simulated responses to human answers from pre-ChatGPT surveys, we offer novel
insights into how participants’ LLM use may impact the content, style, and variance of responses
to online studies.



3     Overview of Analytic Approach and Data

Our analysis proceeds in two parts. Part I provides a descriptive portrait of AI use among online
study participants, drawing on results from an original survey of individuals active on Prolific,
a popular online platform for recruiting study subjects. Part II compares the characteristics of
human vs. LLM-generated open-ended survey responses. Specifically, we examine responses from
three TESS survey questions fielded before the public release of ChatGPT against LLM-generated
answers produced using prompts crafted by participants in our Prolific survey. In what follows, we
describe our primary data sources. We further detail our methods and measures in Analysis Parts I
and II.


3.1       Survey on AI Use

We conducted an original survey to investigate how online survey participants engage with LLMs
when answering open-ended questions. Since the target population for this survey is individuals
who have experience taking online surveys and are likely to do so in the future, we recruited
directly from Prolific, a popular digital platform for sourcing survey and experiment participants.
As online non-probability-based samples continue to be used widely across the social sciences
due to their cost-effectiveness and ease of access (Coppock and McClellan, 2019; Weinberg et al.,
2014), the behavior of online participants – who may also have the strongest motivation for using
AI to help them work more efficiently – is especially important to understand.
    To maximize coverage and variation across key sociodemographic characteristics, we used
Prolific’s “representative sample” recruitment option, which sets quotas based on ethnicity, gen-
der, age, and political affiliation to achieve a sample composition that mirrors that of the U.S.


                                                9
population on these dimensions. While quota sampling approaches remain fundamentally differ-
ent from probability-based sampling, they are often the best available strategy for online studies
and are thus widely used (Yang and Banamah, 2014). We recruited 799 participants to our study;
our final analytic sample excludes two participants who did not give consent, two participants who
did not pass an attention check embedded halfway through the survey, and one participant who
was flagged as a bot by a CAPTCHA test.
   Table 1 shows the characteristics of the sample. Compared to the U.S. population, the sample
has a smaller share of Hispanic/Latino individuals, is more college-educated, and is slightly older.
Almost two-thirds of the participants joined Prolific within the past year, and the vast majority
earn most of their income from sources other than the platform. However, more than 80 percent
of participants reported taking at least three other surveys in the 24-hour period before taking our
survey, suggesting that most are regular survey-takers.

   Our main outcome of interest was the frequency with which participants used LLMs (“AI as-
sistants or chatbots”) for help with answering open-ended survey questions. To minimize social
desirability bias, we framed the survey as a study about online workers’ use of and expertise with
“new AI technologies.” Given that some survey respondents might still feel pressure to misrep-
resent their LLM usage out of reputational concerns, we emphasized that their answers wouldn’t

                                               11
influence their compensation or eligibility for future studies. We also assuaged these concerns by
not collecting Prolific IDs on the survey and reassuring respondents that their personal identifiers
would not be shared with others.
   Additionally, we asked respondents to identify reasons for using (or not using) AI and probed
how they would prompt or talk to AI assistants or chatbots to receive help with writing. To do this,
we showed participants the exact questions seen by TESS participants in the three focal studies.
These respondent-generated prompts provide insight into how survey participants actually engage
with LLMs to generate free-text responses, and we use them to simulate the LLM responses in our
study.


3.2      Data on Human-Generated Survey Responses

For a source of human-crafted answers to open-ended survey questions, we use survey experi-
ment data from three studies conducted via the Time-sharing Experiments for the Social Sciences
(TESS) program, which allows researchers to field studies on NORC’s nationally representative,
probability-based AmeriSpeak panel. All three studies were fielded prior to the public release of
ChatGPT on November 30, 2022. The data for these studies is publicly available through TESS’s
Open Science Framework repository. Table 2 summarizes the questions we used and their sample
sizes. We chose these three surveys because they were among the last waves of TESS studies
administered before ChatGPT’s public release, featured open-ended questions that were reason-
ably easy to isolate and replicate, and addressed topics of theoretical interest to the measurement
of polarization and homogenization in public opinion data. Two of the studies – Jardina et al.
(2021) (JSD for short) and Graham (2021) – explicitly ask about attitudes toward well-defined
social groups and are especially germane to debates about measuring group stereotypes and sen-
sitive topics. The third study, Groenendyk et al. (2025), focuses on a more abstract topic, which
may be more deliberative and cognitively demanding for study participants. Collectively, the three
questions in these studies capture some of the common purposes for open-ended survey questions
in the social sciences.

   Are certain subpopulations of survey participants more likely to use LLMs than others? Sub-
group differences in LLM usage have important consequences for both within-group inferences
and between-group inferences. For example, if members of a social group are more likely to use
LLMs than the rest of the population, their survey responses may be more similar to each other,
creating the false impression that individuals of this group are a homogenous bloc. Moreover,
members of groups with higher LLM usage may hold views that diverge from the rest of the pop-
ulation’s in reality, but produce LLM-generated survey responses that misleadingly bear greater
resemblance to the population average. This effectively suppresses the group’s opinion, which
would bias population-level mean and variance estimates. Table 3 shows how the subset of LLM
users differs from those who say that they never use AI assistants or chatbots for help with an-
swering open-ended questions. In this first snapshot of LLM usage, we find that Black, male,

ended questions. Those newer to the platform – people who started working on Prolific in the past

5.2   Most frequent and distinctive words

Beyond counting the number of words, we also examine which words appear more often in re-
sponses from humans and LLMs. Word frequency tends to follow a highly skewed distribution,
where a small number of words appear much more often than other words. In English, this is
especially true for function words like “the” and “of.” We account for this in two ways. First,
we remove common function words and other similar types of words using a standard list of stop
words. Second, we use term frequency-inverse document frequency (tf-idf) to identify words that
are distinctive to a particular set of responses. This method adjusts for how widely a word appears
across all responses, assigning higher weights to words that are frequent in a single response but
rare overall, and lower weights to words that are common across many responses. This approach
allows us to compare distinctive vocabulary usage between human and LLM responses, highlight-
ing the words that characterize each source’s unique linguistic patterns.

5.4   Use of dehumanizing language

Two of the questions we examine focus on perceptions of social groups: Black people in the JSD
survey and Republicans/Democrats in the Graham study. In this section, we dig more into the
nature of the language that responses employ to describe other people. We focus, in particular, on
the use of dehumanizing language, which has been a subject of broad interest to scholars given the
wide array of social and political concerns that the dehumanization of others raises (Hagan and
Rymond-Richmond, 2008; Haslam and Loughnan, 2014; van Loon et al., 2024).
   Following prior work on measuring dehumanizing language (Mendelsohn et al., 2020), we as-
sess the degree to which the language used to describe social groups reflects moral disgust and
vermin-related metaphors. To do so, we measure semantic similarity between the responses gen-
erated from each source (human, GPT, Claude) and the two concepts (moral disgust and vermin)
using pre-trained GloVe (Global Vectors for Word Representation) word embeddings (Pennington
et al., 2014). Word embeddings represent words as numeric vectors that capture semantic relation-
ships, allowing researchers to quantify the similarity between different words or concepts based on
their usage patterns in existing large text corpora such as Wikipedia (for reviews, see Kozlowski
et al., 2019; Stoltz and Taylor, 2021; Arseniev-Koehler, 2022).
   We constructed word embedding vectors to represent each concept, following the approach in
Mendelsohn et al. (2020). For vermin, we averaged the GloVe word embedding vectors for a list


                                                29
of vermin-related words (e.g. rodent(s), rat(s), cockroaches). For moral disgust, we averaged the
word embedding vectors for the words in the Moral Foundations Dictionary reflecting the concept
(Frimer et al., 2019). The Moral Foundations Dictionary provides a lexicon with which to analyze
text for content related to various moral dimensions, including moral disgust, as elaborated in Haidt
and Graham (2007).
   We then represented each human or LLM-generated response in terms of its average word
embedding. First, we removed stop words, tokenized the text, and calculated term frequencies for
each response. We then loaded the GloVe embeddings (100-dimensional vectors) and computed
the weighted average of its constituent word vectors, with weights proportional to term frequencies.
This process resulted in a single 100-dimensional vector representing each text’s semantic content.
To quantify the similarity between each response and the concepts (vermin, moral disgust), we
calculated the cosine similarity between each text’s average embedding and the vector for each
concept. Cosine similarity in this context provides a measure of semantic closeness, with values
ranging from -1 (perfectly dissimilar) to 1 (perfectly similar).

5.5    Response variation/homogeneity

In this section, we more directly examine whether LLM-generated responses are more homoge-
neous than human-generated ones. We represent each response as a vector of tf-idf scores and then
measure how similar these vectors are to each other using cosine similarity. We calculate the av-
erage pairwise cosine similarity between responses from the same source (i.e., humans to humans,
GPT to GPT) as a proxy for text homogeneity. If the average similarity for responses from a given
source is high (closer to 1), then responses from that source tend to be more homogeneous in their
word choices.

6    Conclusion

Summary of findings: In this paper, we examine how online survey participants use AI tools to
complete open-ended survey questions and compare how LLM-generated responses differ from
answers written by humans. Drawing on an original survey of online survey participants, we
estimate that about 34% of online study participants use LLMs to answer open-ended questions
at least some of the time and find that OpenAI’s ChatGPT is the model of choice for over 90%
of all LLM users. We also find that LLM usage varies by race, gender, education, political party
affiliation, and survey-taking experience, which could compound data quality issues caused by
systematic sampling error.
    Most LLM users reported using AI assistants or chatbots for help with expressing what they
want to say, with few reporting that they use AI because they don’t have any opinions of their
own on the matter. However, in actual prompts submitted by users, the most common strategies
involved asking AI for help with generating ideas or seeking additional information on the topic.
Few prompts explicitly incorporated respondents’ pre-existing views or social positions. This dis-
crepancy raises questions for future research about how participants view ideas generated by AI
during co-writing – do they view them as extensions of their own thoughts, or do they see LLM
suggestions as “new” ideas? Finally, we find that participants who refrain from using AI tools often
cite moral reasons (e.g. AI compromises authenticity, using AI is unethical and akin to “cheating”,
using AI violates rules and terms of services).
    Using TESS survey data collected before the public release of ChatGPT and LLM-generated
answers simulated using respondent-written prompts from our original survey, we find that LLM-
generated responses tend to be longer yet exhibit greater similarity to each other than human-
written responses. Compared to human respondents, LLMs also use more positive language and
are less likely to employ dehumanizing language when discussing social groups. Overall, our re-



                                                  33
sults suggest that even when LLM prompts are customized to specific questions and reflect the
varied prompting strategies of real users, LLM-generated text reflects a narrower range of perspec-
tives and language use than human-written text.
Limitations: This paper has several limitations. One key limitation is that we do not examine actual
AI-assisted survey answers. Instead, we compare human-generated responses to simulations of
AI-generated answers. While we tried to increase external validity by generating LLM responses
using prompts written by real study participants who use AI, participants may still further revise,
selectively use, or reject AI’s suggested answer. There is likely some slippage between the fully AI-
generated responses we examine here and the AI-assisted responses survey-takers would actually
provide. Thus, it may be helpful to interpret our results as reflecting the prospective answers
that participants using AI are exposed to, which some participants may use directly and others
may be steered by (Jakesch et al., 2023). Future research should more directly examine how
study participants engage with AI-generated answers, following the example of other work that
has studied how people interact with AI writing assistance in other types of writing tasks (Lee
et al., 2022; Padmakumar and He, 2023; Sharma et al., 2023; Dhillon et al., 2024; Doshi and
Hauser, 2024).
   Another limitation of this work is that our comparison of LLM- and human-generated answers
relies on matching respondents across samples fielded at different moments in time (2019-2021
for TESS; summer 2024 for our Prolific sample) along a set of important, but limited observable
characteristics. Accordingly, our human-written answers may be imperfect counterfactuals for
LLM-generated answers. For example, views of Black Americans as studied in JSD and Republi-
cans/Democrats in Graham may have evolved over this time period. Nonetheless, such shifts alone
are unlikely to account for many elements of our results, most notably the substantial divergence
in the degree of variation between responses generated by LLMs and humans that we document
consistently across a range of measurement approaches. Future research could further build on
our results by implementing research designs that could provide tighter control, such as fielding a
lab-based study in which participants are monitored and randomly assigned to either have access


                                                 34
to AI tools or not when answering survey questions.
    A further limitation is that our data on AI use and respondent-generated prompts were based
on self-reports. Since participants knew that they were in a study on AI, social desirability bias
and demand effects could have influenced our data. Emerging research suggests that people hide
their AI use to avoid being perceived negatively by others (Slack, 2024), suggesting that our self-
reported usage measures likely underestimate true AI adoption. Nonetheless, future research could
couple self-reports with computational approaches looking for linguistic markers of LLM writing
(e.g. Liang et al., 2024; Veselovsky et al., 2023b), though such strategies may prove less reliable as
models and user behavior continue to evolve. How self-reporting might bias respondent-reported
prompts is more unclear. While a true behavioral measure would have been more externally valid,
it would be far more difficult to obtain and our data on user prompting strategies still offer novel
insights into user behavior. Future research could incorporate better behavioral data, such as digital
archives of prompts or lab studies that track prompting behavior in a more fine-grained manner.
    Our findings are also limited by the particularities of our survey sample. Our survey covers one
commonly used platform – Prolific – but researchers also recruit online participants through other
means like targeted advertisements, mailing lists, and other crowd-working platforms. Existing
research suggests that AI usage among participants on Prolific is actually substantially lower than
on MTurk (Christoforou et al., 2024), but additional comparative research is needed on AI usage
across platforms to inform recruitment strategies for studies where AI poses a significant threat to
validity.
    Finally, our survey only captures a particular snapshot in time. As AI technologies continue
to diffuse rapidly (Bick et al., 2024) and companies increasingly build AI into popular websites,
digital applications, and operating systems, we expect that lowered barriers will lead more survey
participants to use them for writing assistance. Prevalence among subgroups is also likely to shift.
For example, we find that college-educated participants are more likely to use AI than those who
do not have a four-year degree. However, this could be because college-educated knowledge work-
ers are more exposed to LLMs through work and the education gap will disappear or even reverse


                                                 35
as more people engage with AI tools. Patterns of platform churn also play an important role in
determining the future prevalence of AI-generated answers. In our survey, participants who joined
Prolific in the last six months were more likely to use AI tools. If newer participants generally have
a higher tendency to use LLM tools, then their entrance to the platform would continuously drive
up average AI usage, especially if longer-term participants are more likely to exit than newer par-
ticipants. Future research should examine the link between AI adoption and participant selection
into survey platforms, and further explore how platform participants’ LLM usage compares to that
of the broader population.
Contributions: This article offers a portrait of when and why research participants employ AI
when participating in online surveys and experiments. Our survey complements prior work infer-
ring the incidence of LLM usage (Veselovsky et al., 2023a,b; Christoforou et al., 2024) by further
contextualizing such practices in the perspectives and experiences of study participants themselves.
Following other work aiming to draw lessons from the views of crowd-working platform partici-
pants (e.g. Kittur et al., 2013; Fowler et al., 2023; Reynolds and Kincaid, 2024), our study sheds
light on factors that can produce misalignment between researchers’ goals and research subjects’
study participation strategies. By understanding participants’ motivations and constraints, we iden-
tify opportunities for researchers, detailed in the following subsection, to improve both research
data quality and the experiences of online research subjects.
   AI use among crowdworkers, first identified by Veselovsky et al. (2023a,b), has become an in-
creasingly salient concern among machine learning researchers and computational social scientists
who rely on crowdworkers for data annotation. This study, alongside Christoforou et al. (2024),
is among the first to directly elaborate on the implications of this development for social science
surveys and experiments. Unlike data annotation tasks, in which participants provide ground truth
or gold standard examples according to external guidelines – serving more in the role of research
assistants at scale – surveys and experiments are fundamentally aimed at gathering valid infor-
mation on human subjects themselves. This article translates challenges that AI usage raises for
survey and experimental researchers, situating this emerging trend in prior literatures on ensuring


                                                 36
data quality in online samples.
   An important line of research has explored the positions and perspectives that LLMs tend to
output when prompted in different ways. To understand how these LLM tendencies might shape
survey responses, we gathered data on the types of prompts participants report using, showing that
participants rarely prompt AI in the detailed, tailored manner that researchers do to replicate human
responses. Instead, they more often draft more generic prompts, which our simulations show
tend to generate more homogeneous, positive answers to sensitive questions about inter-group
perceptions than human respondents normally write. In so doing, this study adds to scholarship
that bridges research on LLM biases and capabilities with their downstream societal consequences
(e.g. Jakesch et al., 2023; Potter et al., 2024).
Implications and practical recommendations for online research Our findings on why respon-
dents use or avoid AI suggest several strategies to discourage or strategically manage LLM use in
online studies. Many participants cited ethical concerns, a desire for authenticity, and a wish to
follow the rules or researchers’ preferences as reasons for not using AI. This suggests that explic-
itly asking respondents to avoid AI, making normative appeals about the importance of authentic
responses, and fostering participant buy-in could help reduce AI use. Indeed, Veselovsky et al.
(2023a) found that directly asking participants to refrain from using AI reduced its use, especially
when combined with measures that added friction, such as preventing copy-pasting. Researchers
may also consider adding study elements aimed at detecting LLM influence that could be used
to filter responses, such as instructions that exploit known LLM tendencies or weaknesses. We
note, however, that researchers should carefully weigh the trade-offs of such an approach as it may
raise similar issues as traditional attention checks (Berinsky et al., 2014; Muszyński, 2023), such
as falsely flagging genuine human-written responses as AI-produced, confusing respondents, and
introducing selection bias.
   Our results additionally indicate that overly demanding writing tasks or unclear instructions
can prompt participants to turn to AI. To mitigate this, researchers should continue to follow long-
standing best practices, such as thoroughly pretesting and streamlining their studies to reduce re-


                                                    37
spondent burden. As respondents may find the act of writing particularly burdensome (Fowler
et al., 2023), another possibility could be to leverage speech-to-text models to allow respondents
to answer questions orally. Researchers should exercise caution, however, as such models may
fabricate information or introduce sociodemographically disparate transcription errors (Koenecke
et al., 2020, 2024).
   We further emphasize that not all AI use is necessarily detrimental: the most commonly cited
reason for AI use–help with expressing oneself– suggests that AI could be beneficial in some con-
texts. Researchers may consider establishing guidelines for LLM use where it aligns with their
research goals. They may, for instance, encourage respondents to first outline their thoughts in
their own words prior to turning to AI to refine their answers, an approach that could help better
anchor responses in participants’ authentic perspectives. Researchers could also track how AI in-
forms responses by providing space for participants to enter their initial answers and AI-assisted
answers. They could even build AI writing assistance directly into survey and experimental instru-
ments, enabling a more detailed look at how AI shapes responses to their questions. Future studies
should assess which models of AI writing assistance best further researchers’ goals (Lee et al.,
2024). More broadly, future research should evaluate the effectiveness of the various interventions
identified here and clarify the conditions under which they are most productive.
   In drawing implications from our findings, we additionally stress that only 9% of respondents
indicated that they use LLMs “most of the time” or “always” when answering open-ended ques-
tions; the remaining 25% only indicated that they use LLMs “sometimes.” While AI use may in-
crease over time, at the time of writing, we caution against interpreting our findings as undermining
all open-ended text data collected in recent studies. Our results suggest that both the proportion of
and degree to which responses are AI-influenced will vary substantially across studies. Our survey
results suggest that AI use may be more extensive in writing-intensive questions on topics that are
challenging to discuss or where respondents have limited knowledge or weaker opinions.
   To gain a better sense of how AI usage could influence a study, we recommend that researchers
proactively test their open-ended questions by simulating responses using commonly employed AI


                                                 38
models, following the approach we took in this paper. Researchers could examine how often AI
tools refuse to answer their questions and explore what tendencies LLM-generated answers exhibit
in their research setting. With this knowledge, researchers can refine their question phrasing,
anticipate the direction of potential biases introduced by AI assistance, or fine-tune models to detect
AI-generated or AI-influenced responses to their specific questions. Additionally, researchers can
use this information to conduct sensitivity analyses that evaluate how much AI-induced bias would
be required to overturn their findings. We suggest that implementing such measures could become
standard practice amidst the growing take-up of AI assistance, helping producers and evaluators
of research alike more precisely assess whether and how AI use affects the validity of specific
research findings.
   Efforts to align AI systems with human values and train them to avoid producing biased or
harmful content are crucial for managing AI risks and ensuring societal benefit (Kirk et al., 2021;
Ouyang et al., 2022; Kirk et al., 2024). However, these safeguards can have complex consequences
when research participants use AI tools to formulate responses, especially to sensitive questions.
If LLMs are trained to avoid generating content that could be seen as offensive, they may steer
participants’ writing toward more sanitized and less authentic expressions of their opinions and
experiences (Jakesch et al., 2023). This issue may be particularly acute for questions related to
inter-group attitudes and perceptions, which are of significant interest to social scientists. When
LLMs generate responses to such questions, they often lean toward generating more positive and
flattering depictions of social groups, potentially muting ambivalent or negative perspectives. LLM
answers indeed stood in sharp contrast to human responses, which featured a wide range of opin-
ions, including highly derogatory ones. Researchers should consider these AI-induced influences
when designing studies and interpreting their results.
   Our results on the increasing use of AI among online study participants and how it may in-
fluence research findings further point to the growing need for research designs to incorporate
additional validation steps or triangulation between different research methods and data sources.
Indeed, traditional qualitative methods like interviews and ethnography may be more critical than


                                                  39
ever for generating accurate knowledge and understanding of patterns in human beliefs, attitudes,
and experiences (Grigoropoulou and Small, 2022; Small, 2024).
Implications for work on AI evaluation and societal implications of AI: Our finding that a sub-
stantial share of online study participants use generative AI tools to respond to open-ended survey
questions introduces an additional concern for scholars evaluating how well AI is able to predict
or mimic human perspectives: the risk of contamination. If AI-influenced responses are used to
evaluate AI’s ability to replicate human thoughts and judgments, it creates a feedback loop that can
undermine the validity of assessments (Veselovsky et al., 2023b). We might mistakenly conclude
that AI systems are better at reproducing human views than they really are. This issue also extends
to computational social science research that relies on human-labeled data for supervised learning
tasks. If a significant portion of the data used to train these models comes from crowdworking
platform participants who used AI to generate or assist with their responses, it becomes increas-
ingly difficult to establish a true human baseline or “ground truth” against which to evaluate model
performance.
   Furthermore, over the long term, there is a risk of a self-reinforcing cycle, where survey data
and results containing AI-influenced content are used to train models, which in turn power AI
tools that influence the responses of future study participants (Shumailov et al., 2024). Over
time, this could lead to a further homogenization of perspectives and a narrowing of the range
of ideas reflected in research data. Even beyond the problems this would pose for social sci-
ence, these findings also point to everyday implications for the ways that LLMs may standardize
our understandings of the social world (Evans and Foster, 2024; Foster, 2023). To mitigate these
risks, researchers, practitioners, and crowdsourcing platforms should prioritize the development of
methods and standards for detecting, monitoring, and managing AI-influenced writing products.
Cross-disciplinary collaboration will be critical for understanding, addressing, and managing these
emerging challenges.
